Learn what you need to know

A complete sentence needs a subject and a verb and must express a complete thought. Three common errors break this rule.

🧩Fragment: an incomplete sentence. "Because the bus was late." (Because… what?)
🏃Run-on: two sentences jammed together with no punctuation. "I was late the bus broke down."
🔗Comma splice: two sentences joined only by a comma. "I was late, the bus broke down."

Four ways to fix a run-on or comma splice:

1️⃣Period: I was late. The bus broke down.
2️⃣Semicolon: I was late; the bus broke down.
3️⃣Comma + FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so): I was late, for the bus broke down.
4️⃣Subordinate clause: I was late because the bus broke down.

Words like however, therefore and then cannot join two sentences with just a comma. Use a semicolon or a period: "I studied; however, the test was hard."

Fragment fix: attach it to a nearby sentence or add the missing subject or verb.

Key words

  • fragment: a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing a subject, verb or complete thought
  • run-on sentence: two or more sentences joined with no punctuation
  • comma splice: two sentences joined only by a comma
  • FANBOYS: the coordinating conjunctions for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so

See real-life examples

Texting

Messages often use run-ons ("omw be there soon") — fine for friends, not for essays.

Ads

Advertisers use fragments on purpose for punch: "Fresh. Local. Delicious."

Teacher comments

"CS" in the margin of an essay usually means comma splice.
