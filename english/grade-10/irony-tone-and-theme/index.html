Irony, tone and theme

The three kinds of irony, and how irony shapes tone and theme.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 10 English.

Learn what you need to know

Irony is a contrast between what is expected and what actually is.

💬Verbal irony: saying the opposite of what you mean. Sarcasm is a harsh form. ("Lovely weather," during a blizzard.)
🎲Situational irony: the outcome is the opposite of what was expected. (A fire station burns down.)
🎭Dramatic irony: the audience knows something a character does not. (We know Juliet is alive; Romeo does not.)

Irony often creates a tone — humorous, bitter, critical or tragic — and points to the theme. In "The Gift of the Magi," a wife sells her hair to buy a watch chain while her husband sells his watch to buy combs: the situational irony shows that love matters more than possessions.

Satire uses irony and exaggeration to criticize people or society, often to push for change.

Not irony: simple bad luck or coincidence ("rain on your wedding day") is not irony unless it contrasts with an expectation.

Key words

  • verbal irony: saying the opposite of what you mean
  • situational irony: an outcome opposite to what was expected
  • dramatic irony: when the audience knows more than a character
  • satire: using humour, irony and exaggeration to criticize

See real-life examples

Comedy shows

Political satire shows mock leaders through exaggeration.

Short stories

"The Gift of the Magi" by O. Henry is a classic case of situational irony.

Everyday talk

"Great, another Monday!" is usually verbal irony.
