Treaties, the Indian Act and residential schools

Learn what you need to know

To open the West for settlement and the railway, Canada signed Numbered Treaties with First Nations. Treaties 1 to 7 were signed between 1871 and 1877.

First Nations understood treaties as sharing the land and keeping their ways of life, with promises like reserves, schools, farm tools, annual payments and, in Treaty 6, a "medicine chest." The government often saw them as First Nations giving up land. Many promises were broken.

The Indian Act (1876) gave the government control over many parts of First Nations life: who had "status," how reserves were run, and later banned ceremonies such as the potlatch and Sun Dance. It is still in force today, though changed many times.

Residential schools were government-funded, church-run boarding schools. From the 1880s, about 150 000 First Nations, Inuit and Métis children were taken from their families. The goal was to make them give up their languages and cultures. Many suffered abuse, and thousands of children died. The last federally run school closed in 1996.

Survivors have spoken out. In 2008 the federal government apologized. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) issued 94 Calls to Action.

Learning with care: these are painful histories. If they upset you, talk to a trusted adult. The Hope for Wellness Helpline (1-855-242-3310) supports Indigenous people across Canada.

Key words

  • treaty: a formal agreement between nations
  • reserve: land set aside for the use of a First Nation
  • Indian Act: the 1876 federal law controlling many aspects of First Nations life
  • assimilation: forcing a group to give up its culture and adopt another
