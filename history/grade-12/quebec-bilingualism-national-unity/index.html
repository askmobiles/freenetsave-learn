Quebec, bilingualism and national unity

From the Quiet Revolution to two referendums, Quebec's search for its place shaped Canada's identity.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 history.

Learn what you need to know

🌅The Quiet Revolution (1960s): under Jean Lesage, Quebec modernized — the province took over schools and health care from the Church and nationalized hydro-electricity ("Maîtres chez nous")
🗣️Official Languages Act (1969): English and French became equal languages of the federal government
🚨October Crisis (1970): the FLQ kidnapped two officials and killed Quebec minister Pierre Laporte; Ottawa used the War Measures Act
📜Bill 101 (1977): the Charter of the French Language made French the main language of work, signs and schooling in Quebec

Referendums: in 1980 Quebec voters rejected "sovereignty-association" (about 60% No). In 1995 the No side won by a tiny margin — about 50.6% to 49.4%. The Clarity Act (2000) set rules for any future referendum.

Constitution: Quebec did not sign the Constitution Act, 1982. Attempts to bring it in — the Meech Lake Accord (1987–1990) and Charlottetown Accord (1992) — failed.

Identity question: many Quebecers see themselves as a distinct nation inside Canada; in 2006 the House of Commons recognized that "the Québécois form a nation within a united Canada."

Key words

  • Quiet Revolution: Quebec's rapid modernization in the 1960s
  • sovereignty: Quebec governing itself as an independent country
  • referendum: a direct vote by citizens on a single question
  • Bill 101: the 1977 Quebec law making French the main public language
