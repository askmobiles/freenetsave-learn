Residential schools and reconciliation

For over a century, the government and churches took Indigenous children from their families. Learning this history is part of reconciliation.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 history.

Learn what you need to know

Residential schools were funded by the federal government and run mostly by churches. About 150,000 First Nations, Métis and Inuit children attended. Attendance became mandatory for many First Nations children in the 1920s. The last federally funded school closed in 1996.

🚫Children were punished for speaking their languages and separated from siblings
💔Many suffered hunger, disease and abuse; thousands died, and many families were never told
🔁The harm continues across generations (intergenerational trauma)
🍂The "Sixties Scoop": thousands of Indigenous children were taken by child welfare and adopted into non-Indigenous homes

Toward reconciliation: survivors spoke out; the federal government apologized in 2008. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) released its final report in 2015 with 94 Calls to Action. Since 2021, September 30 is the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (Orange Shirt Day).

Reconciliation means building a respectful relationship: telling the truth, honouring treaties, supporting Indigenous languages and self-government, and acting on the Calls to Action.

Key words

  • residential schools: government-funded, mostly church-run schools that removed Indigenous children from their families
  • intergenerational trauma: harm passed from one generation to the next
  • Truth and Reconciliation Commission: the commission that documented residential schools and issued 94 Calls to Action
  • reconciliation: repairing and renewing the relationship between Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples
