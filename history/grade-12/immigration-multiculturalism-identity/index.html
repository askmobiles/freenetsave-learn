Immigration, multiculturalism and identity

Canada changed from a mostly British and French society into one of the world's most diverse — but the path was not always fair.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 history.

Learn what you need to know

🚢Before 1960s: immigration rules favoured Europeans; the Chinese Exclusion Act (1923–1947) almost stopped Chinese immigration
🏕️Second World War: about 22,000 Japanese Canadians were removed from the B.C. coast, their property sold; apology and redress in 1988
🚪1939: Canada refused the MS St. Louis with over 900 Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi Germany
📊1967: the points system judged immigrants on education, skills and language instead of race or country

Multiculturalism: in 1971 Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau announced a multiculturalism policy "within a bilingual framework." The Canadian Multiculturalism Act (1988) made it law, and the Charter (1982) protects equality rights (section 15).

Today about one in four people in Canada was born in another country, and in cities like Toronto more than half of residents are immigrants or their children. Debates continue about housing, jobs, recognizing foreign credentials and anti-racism.

Identity: Canadian identity is often described as a "mosaic" (groups keep their cultures) compared with the American "melting pot".

Key words

  • multiculturalism: a policy that supports cultural diversity within one country
  • points system: a way of choosing immigrants based on skills, education and language
  • redress: making up for a past wrong, often with an apology and payment
  • mosaic: an image of Canada where groups keep their own cultures as part of a whole

See real-life examples

Census

Festivals
