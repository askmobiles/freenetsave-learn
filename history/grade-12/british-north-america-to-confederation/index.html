From British North America to Confederation

How Loyalists, rebellions, responsible government and railway politics led to Confederation in 1867.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 history.

Learn what you need to know

⛪Quebec Act (1774): allowed French civil law and the Catholic religion in Quebec
🧳Loyalists arrived after 1783; the Constitutional Act (1791) split Quebec into Upper and Lower Canada
⚔️War of 1812: British soldiers, Canadian militia and First Nations allies (led by Tecumseh) stopped U.S. invasions
🔥Rebellions of 1837–1838 in Upper and Lower Canada demanded more democratic government

After the rebellions: Lord Durham's report led to the Act of Union (1841), joining the two Canadas. Responsible government — where the cabinet must have the support of the elected assembly — was won in Nova Scotia and the Province of Canada in 1848, through reformers like Joseph Howe, Robert Baldwin and Louis-Hippolyte LaFontaine.

Confederation (July 1, 1867): the British North America Act united Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. Reasons: political deadlock in the Province of Canada, fear of U.S. expansion after the Civil War, the need for railways, and trade after the end of the Reciprocity Treaty.

Fathers of Confederation included John A. Macdonald, George-Étienne Cartier and George Brown. No Indigenous nations or women were part of the negotiations.

Key words

  • Loyalists: colonists loyal to Britain who moved north after the American Revolution
  • responsible government: a system where the cabinet needs the support of the elected assembly
  • Confederation: the union of British North American colonies into Canada in 1867
  • deadlock: a situation where no side can win and government cannot act
