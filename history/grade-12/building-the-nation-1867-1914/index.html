Building the nation, 1867–1914

Canada expanded west through treaties, the railway and immigration — at a heavy cost for Indigenous peoples and some newcomers.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 history.

Learn what you need to know

🏹Red River Resistance (1869–1870): Louis Riel and the Métis formed a provisional government; Manitoba joined Canada in 1870
📜Numbered Treaties 1–11 (1871–1921) with First Nations across the Prairies and North
📕Indian Act (1876): gave the federal government control over many parts of First Nations life
🚂Canadian Pacific Railway finished in 1885; B.C. had joined in 1871 on the promise of a railway

In the North-West Resistance (1885), Métis and some First Nations fought the government; Riel was hanged for treason. Many historians now see Riel as a defender of Métis rights.

Immigration: under Clifford Sifton, about 3 million immigrants arrived from 1896 to 1914, many farming the Prairies (Ukrainians, Germans, Scandinavians). But Chinese workers who helped build the CPR faced a head tax (from $50 in 1885 to $500 in 1903), and in 1914 the Komagata Maru, carrying mostly Sikh passengers, was turned away in Vancouver.

Two stories at once: nation-building connected Canada from sea to sea, while treaties were often misunderstood or broken and racist laws limited who could belong.

Key words

  • Numbered Treaties: eleven treaties signed with First Nations from 1871 to 1921
  • Indian Act: the 1876 law giving Ottawa control over many parts of First Nations life
  • head tax: a fee charged only to Chinese immigrants to discourage them from coming
  • provisional government: a temporary government set up until a permanent one is formed

See real-life examples

Louis Riel Day

Manitoba has a Louis Riel Day holiday in February.

Apologies

Canada apologized for the Chinese head tax in 2006 and for the Komagata Maru in 2016.

Treaty land

Much of Ontario and the Prairies is covered by numbered and other treaties.
