The Wendat, missions and the fur trade

How the fur trade linked First Nations and the French, and how missions and disease changed Wendake.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 5 history.

Learn what you need to know

In Europe, hats made from beaver felt were very popular. The French traded metal pots, knives, axes and cloth for furs from First Nations.

The Wendat (Huron) were skilled traders. They grew lots of corn and traded it with northern nations for furs, then traded the furs with the French. The French and Wendat became allies.

🛶Coureurs de bois: French traders who went inland without permission
🎒Voyageurs: licensed canoe men who carried goods and furs
✝️Jesuits: French priests who came to convert Indigenous people
🏠Sainte-Marie among the Hurons (1639): Jesuit mission near today's Midland, Ontario

Disaster struck. European diseases such as smallpox killed about half of the Wendat in the 1630s. Missions also split communities between Christians and those who kept traditional beliefs.

In 1648–1649 the Haudenosaunee, allied with the Dutch and English, attacked Wendake. The Wendat had to leave their homeland; some moved near Quebec, where Wendake (Wendat community) still exists today.

Both sides gained and lost: trade brought useful goods, but also disease, conflict and dependence.

Key words

  • fur trade: trading furs, especially beaver, for European goods
  • coureurs de bois: "runners of the woods": unlicensed French fur traders
  • missionary: a person sent to spread a religion
  • ally: a partner who agrees to help another side
