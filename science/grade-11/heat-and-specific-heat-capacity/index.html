Heat and specific heat capacity

Learn what you need to know

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles. Thermal energy is their total kinetic energy. Heat is thermal energy transferred from hot to cold, by conduction, convection or radiation.

Q = mcΔT

Q is heat (J), m is mass (g or kg), c is specific heat capacity, and ΔT is the temperature change. Water has a very high c: 4.18 J/(g·°C). Metals are low (copper 0.385, aluminum 0.900).

Example: heating 100 g of water from 20 °C to 30 °C needs Q = 100 × 4.18 × 10 = 4180 J.

Calorimetry: in an insulated container, heat lost by the hot object equals heat gained by the cold one: Q_lost = Q_gained. Latent heat is energy that changes state without changing temperature (Q = mL).

Water's high specific heat moderates coastal climates, makes it a good coolant in car engines, and keeps body temperature stable.

Remember: Q = mcΔT, and water needs a lot of energy to warm up.

Key words

  • specific heat capacity: energy needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C
  • thermal energy: the total kinetic energy of particles
  • calorimetry: measuring heat transfer
  • latent heat: energy that changes state without changing temperature

See real-life examples

Coastal climates

Vancouver's winters are mild because the Pacific Ocean, with water's high specific heat, releases stored heat slowly.

Car radiators

Engine coolant is mostly water because it absorbs a lot of heat for a small temperature rise.

Hot pizza

Pizza sauce burns your mouth more than the crust at the same temperature, because the watery sauce stores more heat.
