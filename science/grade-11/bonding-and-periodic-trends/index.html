Bonding and periodic trends

Atomic radius, ionization energy, electronegativity, and ionic, covalent and polar bonds. Grade 11 science.

📘 Ontario Grade 11 · Chemistry (SCH3U): Matter, Chemical Trends, and Chemical Bonding

Learn what you need to know

Periodic trends come from how strongly the nucleus holds valence electrons.

⭕Atomic radius: decreases across a period (more protons pull harder) and increases down a group (more shells)
⚡Ionization energy (energy to remove an electron): increases across a period, decreases down a group
🧲Electronegativity (attraction for shared electrons): increases across, decreases down; fluorine is highest at 4.0

Bond types depend on the electronegativity difference (ΔEN):

⚡ΔEN greater than 1.7: ionic (electrons transferred), e.g. NaCl (3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1)
↔️ΔEN 0.5 to 1.7: polar covalent (unequal sharing), e.g. H–O in water
🟰ΔEN less than 0.5: non-polar covalent (equal sharing), e.g. H₂, Cl₂

Lewis structures show valence electrons as dots. Shapes like bent (water) and linear (CO₂) decide whether a molecule is polar overall. Water is polar, so it has strong attractions (hydrogen bonds) between molecules, giving it a high boiling point.

Ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct when dissolved or melted. Molecular compounds have lower melting points and usually do not conduct.

Remember: radius gets smaller → and bigger ↓; ionization energy and electronegativity do the opposite.

Key words

  • ionization energy: energy needed to remove an electron from an atom
  • electronegativity: an atom's attraction for shared electrons
  • polar covalent bond: a bond with unequal sharing of electrons
  • Lewis structure: a diagram showing valence electrons as dots

See real-life examples

Water's shape

Water is bent, so its oxygen end is slightly negative and hydrogen ends slightly positive. That is why it dissolves salt so well.

Teflon

The very strong carbon–fluorine bonds in Teflon make non-stick pans resistant to heat and chemicals.

Salt and sugar

Salt (ionic) conducts electricity in water; sugar (molecular) does not, even though both dissolve.
