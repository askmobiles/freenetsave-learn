Nuclear energy

Learn what you need to know

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, such as carbon-12 and carbon-14. Some isotopes are unstable and radioactive: their nuclei decay and give off radiation.

αAlpha: 2 protons + 2 neutrons; stopped by paper
βBeta: a fast electron; stopped by aluminium
γGamma: high-energy light; reduced by thick lead or concrete

Half-life is the time for half of a radioactive sample to decay. After 3 half-lives, 1/8 is left.

Fission: a neutron splits a heavy nucleus (uranium-235) into two smaller nuclei plus 2–3 neutrons and a lot of energy. Those neutrons can split more nuclei: a chain reaction. Ontario's CANDU reactors use fission and make about half of the province's electricity.

Fusion: light nuclei (hydrogen) join to make helium, releasing even more energy. It powers the Sun but is very hard to control on Earth. In both, a little mass becomes energy: E = mc².

Pros and cons: nuclear power makes almost no CO₂, but its used fuel stays radioactive for thousands of years and must be stored safely.

Key words

  • isotope: atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons
  • half-life: the time for half of a radioactive sample to decay
  • fission: splitting a heavy nucleus into smaller ones, releasing energy
  • fusion: joining light nuclei into a heavier one, releasing energy

See real-life examples

Pickering and Bruce

Ontario's nuclear stations use CANDU reactors fuelled by natural uranium and cooled by heavy water.

Smoke detectors

Many smoke alarms use a tiny amount of americium-241, which gives off alpha particles.

Medical scans

Doctors use short half-life isotopes like technetium-99m to see organs from inside the body.
