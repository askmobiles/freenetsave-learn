The mole and chemical amounts

Avogadro's number, molar mass, and converting between mass, moles and particles. Grade 11 science.

📘 Ontario Grade 11 · Chemistry (SCH3U): Quantities in Chemical Reactions

Learn what you need to know

Atoms are too small to count one by one, so chemists count them in moles. One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number), just as a dozen contains 12.

Molar mass (M) is the mass of one mole, in g/mol. Find it by adding atomic masses from the periodic table. H₂O: 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol. CO₂: 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 g/mol. NaCl: 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol.

n = m ÷ M (moles = mass ÷ molar mass)

N = n × 6.02 × 10²³ (number of particles)

Example: 36.04 g of water = 36.04 ÷ 18.02 = 2.00 mol = 2.00 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.20 × 10²⁴ molecules.

Percent composition gives the mass percent of each element: in water, H = 2.02 ÷ 18.02 × 100% ≈ 11.2%, O ≈ 88.8%. An empirical formula is the simplest ratio of atoms; a molecular formula is the actual number (glucose: CH₂O empirical, C₆H₁₂O₆ molecular).

Remember: mass ⇄ moles (÷ or × M) ⇄ particles (× or ÷ 6.02 × 10²³).

Key words

  • mole: 6.02 × 10²³ particles of a substance
  • Avogadro's number: 6.02 × 10²³
  • molar mass: the mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol
  • empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound

See real-life examples

Baking

A cup of sugar (about 200 g) contains about 0.58 mol of sucrose, or 3.5 × 10²³ molecules.

Pharmacies

Medicine doses depend on the amount of a chemical, so drug makers calculate moles precisely.

A mole of water

A mole of water is only 18 g, about one tablespoon, yet it contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.
