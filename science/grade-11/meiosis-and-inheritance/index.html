Meiosis and inheritance

How meiosis makes gametes, creates variation, and what happens when it goes wrong. Grade 11 science.

Learn what you need to know

Meiosis is a special cell division that makes gametes (sperm and egg cells) with half the chromosome number. Human body cells are diploid (2n = 46); gametes are haploid (n = 23). At fertilization, two gametes join to restore 46.

Meiosis has two divisions: Meiosis I separates homologous chromosome pairs; Meiosis II separates sister chromatids (like mitosis). One cell produces four genetically different haploid cells.

🔀Crossing over: homologous chromosomes swap pieces in prophase I
🎲Independent assortment: pairs line up randomly in metaphase I
🧬Random fertilization: any sperm can join any egg

These three processes create huge genetic variation, which helps populations survive change.

Nondisjunction is when chromosomes fail to separate, giving a gamete an extra or missing chromosome. For example, three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21) causes Down syndrome. A karyotype is an image of a person's chromosomes arranged in pairs.

Remember: mitosis → 2 identical diploid cells; meiosis → 4 different haploid cells.

Key words

  • meiosis: cell division that produces haploid gametes
  • haploid: having one set of chromosomes (n)
  • crossing over: exchange of DNA between homologous chromosomes
  • nondisjunction: failure of chromosomes to separate properly

See real-life examples

Siblings

Brothers and sisters (except identical twins) look different because meiosis shuffles genes in a new way each time.

Karyotypes

Genetic counsellors use karyotypes to check chromosome number and structure.

Plant breeding

Crossing over lets breeders combine useful traits, like disease resistance and higher yield, in new crop varieties.
