Ecosystems

Living and non-living parts, and how they interact. Grade 7 science.

📘 Ontario Grade 7 · Interactions in the Environment

1 Learn2 See3 Try4 Play5 Test6 Challenge

Learn what you need to know

An ecosystem is all the living things in an area together with their non-living surroundings.

🦌Biotic factors: living things (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria)
🪨Abiotic factors: non-living things (sunlight, water, soil, air, temperature)

Ecosystems are organized in levels: an organism → a population (one species in an area) → a community (all populations in an area) → an ecosystem (the community plus abiotic factors).

Living things interact. In symbiosis, two species live closely together. In mutualism both benefit (bees and flowers); in commensalism one benefits and the other is not affected; in parasitism one benefits and the other is harmed (ticks on a deer).

Each ecosystem has a carrying capacity: the largest population it can support with its food, water and space.

Remember: ecosystem = biotic + abiotic factors working together.

Key words

  • ecosystem: living things and their non-living surroundings in an area
  • biotic: living
  • abiotic: non-living
  • carrying capacity: the largest population an ecosystem can support

See real-life examples

Algonquin Park

Algonquin Park has forests, lakes and wetlands: many ecosystems with moose, loons, beavers and trees.

Bees and flowers

Bees get nectar; flowers get pollinated. Both win. That is mutualism.

Deer populations

When there are too many deer, they eat all the young plants, and many deer starve in winter. They went past carrying capacity.
