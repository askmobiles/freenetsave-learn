Plate tectonics, earthquakes and volcanoes

How moving plates shape Earth, and how we stay safe in earthquakes.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 8 science.

Learn what you need to know

Earth's outer shell is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates. They move a few centimetres a year, carried by slow currents in the hot mantle below.

⬅️➡️Divergent boundary: plates pull apart; magma rises and makes new crust (mid-Atlantic ridge, Iceland)
➡️⬅️Convergent boundary: plates push together; one may sink under another (subduction), forming mountains and volcanoes
↕️Transform boundary: plates slide past each other (San Andreas Fault)

Earthquakes happen when stress builds up and rocks suddenly break and slip along a fault. The point underground is the focus; the spot above it on the surface is the epicentre. Scientists measure earthquakes with seismometers and describe their size with a magnitude scale.

Volcanoes form where magma reaches the surface, often at plate boundaries and "hot spots" like Hawaii.

British Columbia sits near the Cascadia subduction zone, so it prepares for large earthquakes. In an earthquake: Drop, Cover and Hold On.

Remember: plates move apart, together or past each other, causing earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains.

Key words

  • tectonic plate: a large moving piece of Earth's outer shell
  • subduction: when one plate sinks under another
  • epicentre: the point on the surface above where an earthquake starts
  • seismometer: an instrument that records earthquake waves

Cascadia

Off the coast of British Columbia, an ocean plate is sliding under North America. Scientists watch it closely.

Iceland

Iceland sits on a divergent boundary, so you can walk between the North American and Eurasian plates there.
