Microorganisms and the immune system

Learn what you need to know

Microorganisms (microbes) are living things too small to see without a microscope: bacteria, fungi (like yeast), protists and viruses (which are not cells and can only multiply inside living cells).

🍞Helpful: yeast makes bread rise; bacteria make yogurt and cheese
🌱Helpful: decomposers recycle nutrients in soil
🫃Helpful: gut bacteria help digest food
🤒Harmful: some cause diseases like strep throat, flu and colds

Your immune system defends you. The first line is your skin, mucus, tears and stomach acid. If germs get in, white blood cells attack them. Some make antibodies that lock onto a specific germ, and the body remembers it, so you fight it faster next time.

Vaccines train the immune system safely, so the body is ready if the real germ comes. Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not work on viruses.

Simple habits stop germs spreading: washing hands with soap for 20 seconds, coughing into your elbow, and staying home when sick.

Remember: most microbes are helpful; the immune system and vaccines protect us from harmful ones.

Key words

  • microorganism: a living thing too small to see without a microscope
  • white blood cell: a blood cell that fights germs
  • antibody: a protein that locks onto a specific germ
  • vaccine: something that safely trains the immune system

See real-life examples

Yogurt and bread

Yogurt is made by helpful bacteria, and bread rises because yeast makes gas bubbles.

Penicillin

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, came from a mould discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928.

Hand washing

Washing hands with soap is one of the best ways to stop colds and flu spreading at school.
