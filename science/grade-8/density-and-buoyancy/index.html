Density and buoyancy

Calculating density, and why some things float and others sink. Grade 8 science.

1 Learn2 See3 Try4 Play5 Test6 Challenge

Learn what you need to know

Density is how much mass is packed into a certain volume.

density = mass ÷ volume

Density is often given in grams per cubic centimetre (g/cm³) or grams per millilitre (g/mL). Water has a density of 1 g/mL. Objects denser than water sink; less dense objects float.

Example: a 60 g block with a volume of 20 cm³ has a density of 60 ÷ 20 = 3 g/cm³. It sinks in water.

Buoyancy is the upward push of a fluid on an object. Archimedes' principle says the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid the object pushes aside (displaces). If the buoyant force equals the object's weight, it floats.

A steel ship floats because its hollow shape displaces a huge amount of water, so its overall density (steel plus air) is less than water.

Remember: less dense than the fluid → floats; more dense → sinks.

Key words

  • density: mass divided by volume
  • buoyancy: the upward push of a fluid on an object
  • displace: to push aside a fluid
  • Archimedes' principle: the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid displaced

See real-life examples

Steel ships

Huge steel ships float because their shape holds lots of air and displaces a lot of water.

The Dead Sea

The Dead Sea is so salty and dense that people float easily without swimming.

Submarines

Submarines fill tanks with water to sink and push the water out with air to rise.
