The Sun, Earth and Moon

Learn what you need to know

The Sun is a star powered by nuclear fusion: in its core, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, releasing huge energy. Its surface (photosphere) is about 5500 °C.

The Sun has sunspots (cooler, darker areas), solar flares and coronal mass ejections. Charged particles from the Sun (the solar wind) hit Earth's magnetic field and cause the aurora borealis (northern lights). Strong solar storms can damage satellites and power grids.

The Moon orbits Earth about every 27.3 days, but we see a full cycle of phases every 29.5 days (new, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent). We always see the same side of the Moon.

Tides are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity pulling on Earth's oceans. Most coasts get two high and two low tides each day. Extra-high spring tides happen at new and full moon; smaller neap tides at the quarter moons.

Remember: fusion powers the Sun; the Moon causes phases (by our view) and tides (by gravity).

Key words

  • nuclear fusion: joining small nuclei into bigger ones, releasing energy
  • solar wind: a stream of charged particles from the Sun
  • aurora borealis: the northern lights, caused by solar particles hitting the atmosphere
  • spring tide: an extra-large tide at new or full moon

See real-life examples

Bay of Fundy

The Bay of Fundy between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia has the highest tides in the world, up to about 16 m.

Quebec blackout, 1989

A powerful solar storm in March 1989 knocked out Quebec's power grid for about nine hours.

Northern lights

The aurora is often seen across northern Canada, glowing green and purple.
