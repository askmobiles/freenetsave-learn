Generating electricity

Learn what you need to know

Most electricity is made by generators using electromagnetic induction, discovered by Michael Faraday: moving a magnet near a coil of wire (or a coil near a magnet) produces an electric current.

A turbine spins the generator. Different sources spin the turbine: falling water (hydro), steam from heated water (nuclear, natural gas, coal, biomass), or wind. Solar panels are different: photovoltaic cells turn light directly into electricity with no moving parts.

☢️Nuclear: about half of Ontario's electricity; no CO₂ while running; radioactive waste
💧Hydroelectric: about a quarter; renewable; dams change rivers
🔥Natural gas: flexible for peak times; releases CO₂
💨☀️Wind and solar: renewable, but depend on weather; need backup or storage

Electricity travels from power plants on high-voltage transmission lines, and transformers lower the voltage for homes (120 V and 240 V).

Remember: spin a magnet in a coil to make electricity; each source has trade-offs.

Key words

  • generator: a device that turns kinetic energy into electrical energy
  • electromagnetic induction: producing current by moving a magnet near a wire coil
  • turbine: a wheel spun by water, steam or wind
  • photovoltaic cell: a solar cell that turns light into electricity

See real-life examples

Bruce Power

The Bruce nuclear station on Lake Huron is one of the largest nuclear plants in the world.

Sir Adam Beck stations

The Sir Adam Beck generating stations at Niagara Falls use water from the Niagara River to make hydroelectricity.

Pumped storage

At night, extra electricity can pump water uphill into a reservoir; in the day, it flows down to make power again.
