Environmental chemistry

How chemicals move through air, water, soil and living things, and how we measure and reduce pollution.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 9 science.

Learn what you need to know

Living things need nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. But too much of a substance, or the wrong one, becomes a pollutant. "The dose makes the poison."

🌧️Acid rain: sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuel mix with rain and make it acidic.
🐟Biomagnification: mercury or DDT builds up as it moves up a food chain, so top predators get the most.
🛢️Spills and leaks: oil, road salt and fertilizer can soak into soil and water.

Measuring: scientists test water for pH (normal rain is about 5.6; acid rain is lower), dissolved oxygen, and tiny amounts in parts per million (ppm). Living indicator species, like mayfly larvae, show clean water.

Breaking down: some substances are biodegradable and microbes break them down. Others, like plastics, DDT and heavy metals, last a long time. Bioremediation uses microbes or plants to clean polluted sites.

Canada acted: scrubbers and cleaner fuels cut acid rain in the 1990s, and DDT was banned in the 1970s after it thinned the eggshells of birds of prey.

Key words

  • pollutant: a substance that harms the environment or living things
  • biomagnification: when a chemical becomes more concentrated higher in a food chain
  • ppm: parts per million, a way to measure very small amounts
  • biodegradable: able to be broken down by living things like bacteria
