Stars and galaxies

Learn what you need to know

Stars are born in nebulae. Gravity pulls gas together until the core is hot enough for fusion, forming a main sequence star like our Sun.

🟡Medium stars like the Sun: main sequence → red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf
🔵Massive stars: main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole

The Hertzsprung–Russell (H-R) diagram plots stars by temperature and brightness (luminosity). Most stars, including the Sun, fall on the main sequence band. Hot stars are blue-white; cool stars are red.

A galaxy is a huge group of stars, gas and dust held by gravity. Types include spiral (like the Milky Way), elliptical and irregular. Most large galaxies have a supermassive black hole at the centre.

The Big Bang theory says the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since. Evidence: galaxies moving away from us (redshift) and the cosmic microwave background radiation.

Remember: a star's mass decides its life; the universe began 13.8 billion years ago.

Key words

  • main sequence: the stable, longest stage of a star's life
  • supernova: the huge explosion of a massive star
  • black hole: a region where gravity is so strong that not even light escapes
  • redshift: stretching of light from objects moving away from us

See real-life examples

Betelgeuse

Betelgeuse in Orion is a red supergiant. It will explode as a supernova sometime in the next 100 000 years.

The 1987 supernova

In 1987, Canadian astronomer Ian Shelton was among the first to see Supernova 1987A, the brightest in centuries.

Black hole photo

In 2019, scientists took the first image of a black hole's shadow, in the galaxy M87.
