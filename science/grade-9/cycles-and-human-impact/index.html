Cycles and human impact

Learn what you need to know

Matter cycles through biogeochemical cycles: through living things (bio), rocks and soil (geo) and chemical changes.

🌳Carbon cycle: photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration, decomposition and combustion return it; oceans and rocks store it
🌾Nitrogen cycle: nitrogen-fixing bacteria turn N₂ gas into forms plants use; denitrifying bacteria return it to the air
🪨Phosphorus cycle: phosphorus comes from rock weathering; there is no gas form, so it cycles slowly through soil and water

Humans change these cycles:

Burning fossil fuels releases carbon that was stored for millions of years, raising CO₂ in the air and causing climate change. Deforestation removes carbon sinks. Fertilizers add extra nitrogen and phosphorus; runoff causes eutrophication: algae blooms that use up oxygen and create "dead zones".

Solutions include renewable energy, protecting forests and wetlands, buffer strips of plants along streams, and using fertilizer carefully.

Remember: carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus cycle naturally; human activities add too much too fast.

Key words

  • combustion: burning, which releases carbon dioxide
  • nitrogen fixation: bacteria changing nitrogen gas into usable forms
  • eutrophication: excess nutrients causing algae blooms and low oxygen
  • carbon sink: something that stores more carbon than it releases

See real-life examples

Lake Erie dead zone

Phosphorus from farms and sewage feeds algae in Lake Erie. When algae die and rot, oxygen drops and fish die.

Legumes

Beans, peas and clover have bacteria in root nodules that fix nitrogen, so farmers rotate them to enrich soil.

Experimental Lakes Area

In northwestern Ontario, scientists added phosphorus to a test lake in the 1970s and proved it caused algae blooms. This led to phosphate bans in laundry soap.
