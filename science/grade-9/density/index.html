Density

Calculating density, rearranging the formula, and using density to identify substances. Grade 9 science.

📘 Ontario Grade 9 · Chemistry: The Nature of Matter

Learn what you need to know

Density is mass per unit volume.

D = m ÷ V so m = D × V and V = m ÷ D

Units: g/cm³ for solids, g/mL for liquids (1 mL = 1 cm³), and kg/m³ for gases and large objects. Water is 1.0 g/mL.

Measure the volume of a regular solid with length × width × height. For an irregular solid, use water displacement: the rise in water level in a graduated cylinder equals the object's volume.

Density is a characteristic physical property: it does not depend on the amount, so it helps identify substances. Aluminum is 2.7 g/cm³, iron 7.9 g/cm³, gold 19.3 g/cm³.

Density usually decreases when a substance is heated (it expands). Water is unusual: ice (0.92 g/cm³) is less dense than liquid water, so lakes freeze from the top down and fish survive winter.

Remember: D = m ÷ V, and the same substance always has the same density at the same temperature.

Key words

  • characteristic property: a property that identifies a substance, no matter the amount
  • water displacement: measuring volume by how much water an object pushes up
  • graduated cylinder: a tall container for measuring liquid volume
  • meniscus: the curved surface of a liquid in a tube

See real-life examples

Frozen lakes

Because ice is less dense than water, Canadian lakes freeze from the top. The ice layer insulates the water below so fish survive.

Gold rushes

Gold panners swirl gravel in water. Dense gold sinks to the bottom while lighter sand washes away.

Hot-air balloons

Heated air is less dense than the cool air around it, so the balloon rises.
