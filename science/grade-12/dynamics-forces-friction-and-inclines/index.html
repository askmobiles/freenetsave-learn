Dynamics: forces, friction and inclines

Newton's laws in two dimensions, free-body diagrams, friction, ramps, pulleys and connected objects.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 science.

Learn what you need to know

Newton's laws: (1) an object keeps its velocity unless a net force acts; (2) Fnet = ma; (3) forces come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects.

Always start with a free-body diagram: draw every force on the object (gravity Fg = mg, normal force FN, friction Ff, tension FT, applied force). Pick axes, split forces into components, then apply ΣF = ma on each axis.

🧱Static friction: F<sub>s</sub> ≤ μ<sub>s</sub>F<sub>N</sub>, stops sliding from starting
🛷Kinetic friction: F<sub>k</sub> = μ<sub>k</sub>F<sub>N</sub>, acts while sliding; usually μ<sub>k</sub> &lt; μ<sub>s</sub>
⛰️On a ramp: gravity down the slope = mg sinθ; into the slope = mg cosθ, so F<sub>N</sub> = mg cosθ
🪢Connected objects: same acceleration; treat the whole system first, then one object to find tension

Frictionless ramp: a = g sinθ. With friction, sliding down: a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ). In an Atwood machine (two masses over a pulley), a = (m₁ − m₂)g ÷ (m₁ + m₂).

In an accelerating frame of reference (like a turning car or an elevator), you feel "fictitious" forces. In an elevator accelerating up, the scale reads more than your weight: FN = m(g + a).

Remember: draw the free-body diagram first, choose axes along the motion, then ΣF = ma for each axis.

Key words

  • free-body diagram: a sketch showing every force acting on one object
  • normal force: the push of a surface, perpendicular to it
  • coefficient of friction (μ): a number comparing friction to the normal force
  • inertial frame: a frame of reference that is not accelerating

See real-life examples

Winter driving

Ice has a much lower μ than dry asphalt, so cars need far longer to stop on Canadian winter roads.

Elevators

You feel heavier when an elevator starts going up and lighter when it starts going down.

Ski hills

A skier on a steeper slope speeds up faster because more of gravity points down the slope.
