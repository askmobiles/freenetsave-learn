Learn what you need to know

Around 1900, Max Planck explained the light from hot objects (blackbody radiation) by assuming energy comes in packets, quanta: E = hf, where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

In the photoelectric effect, light shining on a metal knocks out electrons. Einstein (1905) explained it with light photons: one photon gives all its energy to one electron. Ek,max = hf − W, where W is the metal's work function.

🚫Below the threshold frequency, no electrons leave, however bright the light
🔆Brighter light (above threshold) means more photons, so more electrons, same maximum energy
🌈Higher frequency means more energetic electrons
⚡No time delay: electrons leave instantly

Wave–particle duality: light acts as a wave (interference) and as particles (photons). De Broglie said matter has waves too: λ = h ÷ p. Electron diffraction (Davisson–Germer) confirmed it, and electron microscopes use it.

Bohr model: electrons in atoms have only certain energy levels. When an electron drops to a lower level it emits a photon with E = Ehigh − Elow, so each element has its own line spectrum. The Compton effect showed photons carry momentum too, p = h/λ.

Remember: E = hf, Ek = hf − W, λ = h/p. Intensity controls how many; frequency controls how energetic.

Key words

  • photon: a packet (quantum) of light energy
  • work function: the minimum energy needed to free an electron from a metal
  • threshold frequency: the lowest light frequency that can free electrons
  • wave–particle duality: light and matter show both wave and particle behaviour

See real-life examples

Solar panels

Photons free electrons in silicon cells, turning sunlight into electricity.

Automatic doors and cameras

Light sensors and camera chips count photons to detect light.

Electron microscopes

Fast electrons have tiny wavelengths, so they show details far smaller than light microscopes can.
