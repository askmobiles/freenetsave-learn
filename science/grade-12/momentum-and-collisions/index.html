Momentum and collisions

Learn what you need to know

Momentum p = mv (kg·m/s) is a vector. Impulse J = FΔt equals the change in momentum, Δp. The area under a force–time graph is the impulse.

Spreading a stop over a longer time lowers the force for the same change in momentum. That is why airbags, crumple zones, bent knees and helmets reduce injuries.

🔒Conservation of momentum: in an isolated system, total momentum before = total momentum after
🎱Elastic collision: momentum and kinetic energy both conserved
🚗Inelastic collision: momentum conserved, some kinetic energy lost
🧲Perfectly inelastic: objects stick together; most kinetic energy lost

In two dimensions, momentum is conserved separately in x and y. Split each momentum into components, add them, then rebuild the final vector with Pythagoras and tan⁻¹.

Recoil and rockets also follow conservation: when a cannon fires a ball forward, the cannon moves backward so the total momentum stays zero. Rockets push gas backward to move forward, even in space.

Remember: momentum is always conserved in an isolated collision; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions.

Key words

  • momentum: mass times velocity
  • impulse: force times time; equal to the change in momentum
  • elastic collision: a collision where kinetic energy is conserved
  • isolated system: a system with no net external force

See real-life examples

Hockey

A slapshot gives the puck a big impulse: a large force over a short contact time.

Car safety

Crumple zones make a crash last longer, so the force on passengers is smaller.

Rockets

Rockets push exhaust gas backward at high speed, and gain an equal momentum forward.
