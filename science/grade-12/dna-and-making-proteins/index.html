DNA and making proteins

Learn what you need to know

DNA is a double helix: two strands of nucleotides with a sugar–phosphate backbone. Bases pair A–T and C–G with hydrogen bonds. The two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel, 5′→3′ and 3′→5′).

Replication is semi-conservative: each new DNA has one old and one new strand. Helicase unzips, DNA polymerase adds nucleotides 5′→3′, and ligase joins the Okazaki fragments of the lagging strand.

📜Transcription (nucleus): DNA → mRNA by RNA polymerase
✂️RNA processing: introns cut out, exons joined, cap and poly-A tail added
🏭Translation (ribosome): mRNA → protein
🔤Codon: 3 bases code for 1 amino acid; AUG = start, UAA/UAG/UGA = stop

In RNA, uracil (U) replaces thymine. tRNA carries amino acids and has an anticodon that pairs with the mRNA codon.

A mutation is a change in DNA. A substitution may be silent, missense or nonsense. An insertion or deletion causes a frameshift, which usually changes every amino acid after it.

Remember: DNA → RNA → protein (the central dogma). A pairs with T in DNA, A pairs with U in RNA.

Key words

  • codon: three mRNA bases that code for one amino acid
  • transcription: making mRNA from a DNA template
  • translation: making a protein from mRNA at a ribosome
  • frameshift: a mutation that shifts the reading frame of codons

See real-life examples

mRNA vaccines

Some COVID-19 vaccines give cells mRNA, and the cells translate it into a harmless virus protein that trains the immune system.

Sickle cell

One base substitution in the haemoglobin gene changes one amino acid, which makes red blood cells bend into a sickle shape.

DNA testing

Forensic labs copy tiny amounts of DNA with PCR, which uses the same base-pairing rules as replication.
