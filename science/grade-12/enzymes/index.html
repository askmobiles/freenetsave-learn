Enzymes

Learn what you need to know

Enzymes are biological catalysts, almost all proteins. They speed up reactions by lowering the activation energy, without being used up and without changing ΔG (the energy difference between reactants and products).

The substrate binds at the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex. In the induced-fit model, the active site changes shape slightly to fit the substrate tightly (better than the older lock-and-key model).

🌡️Temperature: rate rises to an optimum, then falls as the enzyme denatures
🧪pH: each enzyme has an optimum (pepsin about 2, trypsin about 8)
📈Substrate concentration: rate rises, then levels off when all active sites are busy (saturation)
🧲Cofactors (metal ions like Mg²⁺) and coenzymes (vitamins, NAD⁺) help some enzymes

Competitive inhibitors look like the substrate and block the active site; more substrate can overcome them. Non-competitive (allosteric) inhibitors bind elsewhere and change the active site shape; more substrate does not help.

Feedback inhibition: the final product of a pathway inhibits an early enzyme, so the cell does not make too much. Many drugs and poisons act as enzyme inhibitors (for example, penicillin blocks a bacterial enzyme that builds cell walls).

Remember: enzymes lower activation energy, are specific, are reused, and work best at their optimum temperature and pH.

Key words

  • active site: the region of an enzyme where the substrate binds
  • induced fit: the active site changes shape to grip the substrate
  • allosteric site: a site away from the active site where molecules can bind to regulate an enzyme
  • coenzyme: an organic helper molecule, often from a vitamin

See real-life examples

Laundry detergent

Biological detergents contain proteases and lipases that break down protein and fat stains at low temperatures.

Penicillin

Penicillin blocks a bacterial enzyme that builds cell walls, so bacteria burst.

Pineapple and gelatin

Fresh pineapple has bromelain, a protease that stops gelatin desserts from setting; canned pineapple has been heated, which denatures it.
