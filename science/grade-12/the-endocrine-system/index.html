The endocrine system

Learn what you need to know

The endocrine system uses hormones: chemical messengers released into the blood by glands. Hormones act more slowly than nerves but their effects last longer. Only target cells with the right receptors respond.

Steroid hormones (made from cholesterol, like testosterone and cortisol) are lipid-soluble: they pass through the membrane and act on genes inside the cell. Protein (peptide) hormones (like insulin) bind receptors on the surface and use a second messenger such as cAMP.

🧠Hypothalamus → pituitary: releasing hormones control the "master gland"; posterior pituitary releases ADH and oxytocin
🦋Thyroid: thyroxine raises metabolic rate; TSH from the pituitary controls it; iodine is needed
🍬Pancreas: insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it
⚡Adrenal glands: adrenaline (short-term stress), cortisol (long-term stress), aldosterone (salt and water)

Most hormones are controlled by negative feedback: high thyroxine tells the hypothalamus and pituitary to make less TRH and TSH. Diabetes: type 1 = the pancreas makes no insulin; type 2 = cells stop responding well to insulin.

Reproductive hormones: FSH and LH from the pituitary control the ovaries and testes. Estrogen and progesterone run the menstrual cycle (a surge of LH triggers ovulation); testosterone controls sperm production and male traits.

Remember: nerves are fast and short; hormones are slow and long-lasting. Insulin lowers sugar, glucagon raises it.

Key words

  • hormone: a chemical messenger carried in the blood
  • target cell: a cell with receptors for a particular hormone
  • second messenger: a molecule inside a cell (like cAMP) that relays a hormone signal
  • negative feedback: a response that reverses a change to restore balance
