DNA structure and replication

Learn what you need to know

Key discoveries: Griffith (transformation, 1928), Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (DNA is the transforming factor), Hershey and Chase (DNA, not protein, enters cells from viruses), Chargaff (A = T, C = G), Rosalind Franklin (X-ray photo 51) and Watson and Crick (double helix, 1953).

DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel strands (one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′). The sugar–phosphate backbone is on the outside; bases pair inside: A–T (2 H-bonds) and C–G (3 H-bonds). Purines (A, G) pair with pyrimidines (T, C).

🧷Helicase unwinds and unzips the helix at the replication fork
📌Primase lays down a short RNA primer
🧱DNA polymerase III adds nucleotides in the 5′→3′ direction only
🧵DNA polymerase I replaces primers; ligase joins Okazaki fragments

The leading strand is made continuously toward the fork. The lagging strand is made in short pieces (Okazaki fragments) away from the fork. Single-strand binding proteins keep strands apart and topoisomerase (gyrase) relieves twisting.

Replication is semi-conservative (Meselson and Stahl, 1958): each new DNA has one old and one new strand. DNA polymerase proofreads, so errors are very rare (about 1 in a billion bases). Telomeres at chromosome ends shorten with each division.

Remember: helicase → primase → polymerase III → polymerase I → ligase. New DNA is always built 5′→3′.

Key words

  • antiparallel: running in opposite directions
  • Okazaki fragment: a short piece of DNA made on the lagging strand
  • semi-conservative: each new DNA keeps one original strand
  • primer: a short RNA starting point for DNA polymerase
