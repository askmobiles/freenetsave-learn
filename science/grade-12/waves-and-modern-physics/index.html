Waves and modern physics

Learn what you need to know

Light shows wave behaviour. In Young's double-slit experiment, light from two slits makes bright and dark bands (fringes). Bright bands are constructive interference; dark bands are destructive.

🌈Diffraction: waves spread through a gap; more when the gap ≈ the wavelength
🕶️Polarization: only transverse waves can be polarized, so light is transverse
🫧Thin films: soap bubbles show colours from interference
📏Double slit: fringe spacing Δy = λL/d

Light also acts as particles called photons, each with energy E = hf (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s). In the photoelectric effect, light above a threshold frequency knocks electrons off a metal; brighter light gives more electrons, but higher frequency gives faster ones (Einstein, 1905).

Matter has waves too: the de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p. Electrons make diffraction patterns, which is how electron microscopes see tiny things. This is wave–particle duality.

The Bohr model says electrons have set energy levels; atoms emit or absorb photons of exact energies when electrons jump, giving each element its own line spectrum.

Remember: E = hf. Brightness = how many photons; frequency = energy per photon.

Key words

  • interference: waves adding or cancelling where they meet
  • diffraction: waves spreading through a gap or around an edge
  • photon: a particle (packet) of light energy
  • photoelectric effect: light knocking electrons out of a metal

See real-life examples

Sunglasses

Polarized sunglasses block glare because reflected light off water or roads is partly polarized.

Solar panels

Solar cells use photons to free electrons, a cousin of the photoelectric effect.

Neon signs

Each gas glows with its own colours (a line spectrum) because its electrons jump between set energy levels.
