Electric fields and forces

Learn what you need to know

Coulomb's law: two point charges exert F = kq₁q₂ ÷ r², with k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. Like gravity, it is an inverse-square law, but much stronger and can repel.

An electric field is the region where a charge feels a force. Field strength ε (or E) = F ÷ q (N/C). Around a point charge, E = kq ÷ r². Field lines point away from positive and toward negative charges; closer lines mean a stronger field.

🔋Parallel plates: uniform field E = ΔV ÷ d between them
⚡Electric potential V = E<sub>p</sub> ÷ q (volts); a volt is a joule per coulomb
🔢Charge is quantized: every charge is a whole multiple of e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
🛢️Millikan balanced charged oil drops between plates to measure e (1909)

A charge q moving through a potential difference ΔV gains or loses energy qΔV. An electron accelerated through 1 V gains 1 electron-volt (1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J). This is how cathode-ray tubes and particle accelerators speed up particles.

Inside a conductor in equilibrium the electric field is zero, so a metal car or a Faraday cage protects people inside from lightning.

Remember: F = kq₁q₂/r², E = F/q, E = ΔV/d for plates, field lines go + → −.

Key words

  • electric field: the region around a charge where other charges feel a force
  • electric potential: electric potential energy per unit charge, in volts
  • elementary charge: the charge of one proton or electron, 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • Faraday cage: a metal enclosure that blocks outside electric fields

See real-life examples

Photocopiers and laser printers

Charged drums attract toner powder in the shape of the text.

Lightning safety

A car's metal body acts as a Faraday cage, so the charge flows around the outside.

Air cleaners

Electrostatic precipitators charge smoke particles so they stick to oppositely charged plates.
