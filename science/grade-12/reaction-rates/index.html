Reaction rates

Learn what you need to know

The rate of reaction is how fast reactants are used up or products form: rate = change in concentration ÷ time (mol/(L·s)). It can be measured by gas made, mass lost, colour change or pH.

Collision theory: particles must collide with the right orientation and with at least the activation energy (Eₐ). At the top of the energy hill is the activated complex (transition state).

📈Concentration or pressure up → more collisions
🌡️Temperature up → particles faster, many more have Eₐ (about ×2 per 10 °C)
🔪Surface area up (powder) → more particles exposed
⚗️Catalyst → a new pathway with lower Eₐ; not used up

The rate law: rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. The orders m and n come from experiments, not from the balanced equation. If doubling [A] doubles the rate, it is first order in A; if it quadruples the rate, it is second order.

A reaction mechanism is the series of elementary steps. The slowest step is the rate-determining step. Intermediates are made in one step and used in a later one; catalysts are used then regenerated.

Remember: more effective collisions = faster rate. Catalysts lower Eₐ but do not change ΔH.

Key words

  • activation energy: the minimum energy needed for a collision to react
  • activated complex: the unstable arrangement at the top of the energy barrier
  • rate-determining step: the slowest step in a mechanism
  • rate constant (k): the number in a rate law that depends on temperature

See real-life examples

Fridges

Cold slows the reactions that spoil food.

Catalytic converters

Platinum and palladium in car exhausts speed up turning CO and NOₓ into CO₂ and N₂.

Flour dust

Fine flour or sawdust in the air can explode because of its huge surface area.
