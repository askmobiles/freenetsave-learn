Gravitational fields and orbits

Learn what you need to know

Newton's law of universal gravitation: every mass attracts every other mass with F = Gm₁m₂ ÷ r², where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² and r is the distance between centres. It is an inverse-square law: double the distance and the force becomes ¼.

A gravitational field is the region around a mass where other masses feel a force. Field strength g = F ÷ m = GM ÷ r² (N/kg). At Earth's surface g ≈ 9.8 N/kg; it gets weaker with height.

🛰️Orbital speed: gravity provides the centripetal force, GMm/r² = mv²/r, so v = √(GM/r)
⏱️Period of orbit: T = 2πr ÷ v; higher orbits are slower and take longer
📡Geostationary satellites: period 24 h, stay above one spot on the equator (about 36 000 km up)
🚀Escape speed: v = √(2GM/r) ≈ 11.2 km/s from Earth's surface

Kepler's laws: (1) planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus; (2) a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (faster when closer); (3) T² ∝ r³ for all planets around the same star.

Astronauts on the International Space Station feel weightless not because there is no gravity (g is still about 8.7 N/kg there) but because they are in free fall around Earth along with the station.

Remember: F = Gm₁m₂/r², g = GM/r², vorbit = √(GM/r). Distance is measured from the centre.

Key words

  • gravitational field: the region around a mass where other masses feel a force
  • inverse-square law: a quantity that falls as 1 ÷ distance²
  • geostationary orbit: an orbit that keeps a satellite above the same spot on Earth
  • escape speed: the minimum speed to leave a planet without further push

See real-life examples

Satellite TV and GPS

Geostationary satellites stay over one place for TV; GPS satellites orbit lower and faster.

Canadarm

Canada's robotic arms on the space shuttle and space station work in free-fall orbit.

Tides

The Moon's gravity pulls more on the near side of Earth than the far side, causing ocean tides.
