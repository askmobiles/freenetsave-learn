Learn what you need to know

Bohr explained hydrogen's line spectrum: electrons sit in fixed energy levels and emit a photon of one exact energy when they drop to a lower level. De Broglie showed electrons act like waves, Heisenberg showed we cannot know an electron's exact position and momentum together, and Schrödinger described electrons as orbitals: regions where an electron is likely to be found.

nPrincipal (n = 1, 2, 3…): energy level and size
lSecondary (l = 0 to n−1): shape — s, p, d, f
mₗMagnetic (−l to +l): orientation; s has 1 orbital, p 3, d 5, f 7
mₛSpin (+½ or −½): each orbital holds 2 electrons with opposite spin

Filling rules: aufbau (lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p…), Pauli exclusion (no two electrons share all four quantum numbers), Hund's rule (in equal-energy orbitals, fill singly before pairing). Exceptions: Cr is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ and Cu is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰.

The periodic table has s, p, d and f blocks. Trends: atomic radius decreases across a period (more protons pull harder) and increases down a group (more shells). Ionization energy and electronegativity do the opposite: they increase across and decrease down.

Remember: s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10, f holds 14. Fill low to high, one per box first, opposite spins in pairs.

Key words

  • orbital: a region of space where an electron is likely to be found
  • quantum number: one of four numbers that describe an electron's state
  • Hund's rule: electrons fill equal orbitals singly before pairing
  • ionization energy: the energy to remove an electron from a gas atom

See real-life examples

Neon signs

Each gas glows a different colour because its electrons drop between different energy levels.

Fireworks

Strontium gives red and copper gives blue-green, from their unique emission lines.

MRI scans

MRI machines use the spin of hydrogen nuclei in the body to make pictures.
