Uniform circular motion

Centripetal acceleration and force, period and frequency, banked curves, vertical circles and artificial gravity.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 science.

Learn what you need to know

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating, because the direction of its velocity keeps changing. The acceleration points to the centre: centripetal acceleration.

🎯a<sub>c</sub> = v² ÷ r, pointing to the centre
🧲F<sub>c</sub> = mv² ÷ r: a net force toward the centre is needed
⏱️v = 2πr ÷ T, where T is the period (time for one turn)
🔁a<sub>c</sub> = 4π²r ÷ T² and frequency f = 1 ÷ T

Centripetal force is not a new force; it is whatever force points to the centre: friction for a car on a flat curve, tension for a ball on a string, gravity for the Moon, the normal force on a banked curve.

If the centripetal force disappears, the object moves off in a straight line tangent to the circle (it does not fly straight outward). The "outward pull" you feel in a turning car is your inertia: a centrifugal effect in a non-inertial frame.

On a banked curve with no friction, tanθ = v² ÷ (rg). In a vertical loop, the minimum speed at the top is v = √(gr), when gravity alone provides the centripetal force.

Remember: velocity is tangent, acceleration and net force point to the centre.

Key words

  • centripetal: toward the centre
  • period (T): the time for one complete turn
  • tangent: a line that just touches the circle at one point
  • banked curve: a road tilted inward to help cars turn

See real-life examples

Highway ramps

Curved on-ramps are banked so the road helps push cars toward the centre of the turn.

Salad spinners

The basket pushes the lettuce in a circle, but water drops are not held and fly off along tangents.

Space stations

A spinning space station could create artificial gravity, with the floor pushing people toward the centre.
