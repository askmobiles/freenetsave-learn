Work, energy and simple harmonic motion

The work–energy theorem, conservation of energy, Hooke's law, elastic potential energy and simple harmonic motion.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 science.

Learn what you need to know

Work W = Fd cosθ (in joules). The work–energy theorem: the net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, Wnet = ΔEk.

🏃Kinetic energy E<sub>k</sub> = ½mv²
⛰️Gravitational potential energy E<sub>g</sub> = mgh
🌀Elastic potential energy E<sub>e</sub> = ½kx²
🔥Friction turns mechanical energy into thermal energy

Conservation of energy: in an isolated system, total energy stays the same. With no friction, Ek + Eg + Ee is constant, so a roller coaster speeds up as it goes down.

Hooke's law: a spring's restoring force is F = −kx, where k is the spring constant (N/m) and x the stretch or compression from equilibrium. The minus sign means the force points back toward equilibrium.

A mass on a spring moves in simple harmonic motion (SHM): it oscillates about equilibrium with period T = 2π√(m/k), which does not depend on the amplitude. Speed is greatest at equilibrium; acceleration is greatest at the ends. Damping (friction) slowly reduces the amplitude.

Remember: energy changes form but the total stays the same. Springs: F = kx, E = ½kx², T = 2π√(m/k).

Key words

  • work: energy transferred by a force acting through a distance
  • spring constant (k): how stiff a spring is, in N/m
  • amplitude: the largest distance from equilibrium in an oscillation
  • damping: loss of energy that makes oscillations die down

See real-life examples

Car suspension

Springs and shock absorbers (dampers) keep a car from bouncing up and down after a bump.

Bungee jumping

Gravitational energy turns into kinetic energy, then into elastic energy in the stretched cord.

Pendulum clocks

A pendulum swings in nearly simple harmonic motion, with a steady period that keeps time.
