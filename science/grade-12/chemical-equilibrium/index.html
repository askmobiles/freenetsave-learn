Chemical equilibrium

Learn what you need to know

In a closed system, a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium: the forward and reverse rates are equal, so concentrations stay constant, even though both reactions keep happening.

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant K = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ ÷ [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Pure solids and liquids are left out. K > 1: products favoured; K < 1: reactants favoured. K changes only with temperature.

The reaction quotient Q uses the same formula with current concentrations. If Q < K, the reaction shifts right; if Q > K, it shifts left; if Q = K, it is at equilibrium. ICE tables (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) help find unknown concentrations.

➕Add reactant or remove product → shifts right
🗜️Increase pressure (smaller volume) → shifts to the side with fewer gas moles
🔥Heat an endothermic reaction → shifts right and K increases
⚗️Catalyst → reaches equilibrium faster, no shift

Solubility equilibrium: for AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺ + Cl⁻, Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. If the ion product is bigger than Ksp, a precipitate forms. Adding a common ion (like Cl⁻ from NaCl) lowers solubility: the common ion effect.

Remember: Le Châtelier: the system shifts to undo the change. Only temperature changes K.

Key words

  • dynamic equilibrium: forward and reverse rates equal; concentrations constant
  • equilibrium constant (K): the ratio of product to reactant concentrations at equilibrium
  • Le Châtelier's principle: a system at equilibrium shifts to reduce a change
  • Ksp: the solubility product constant

See real-life examples

Haber process

Ammonia for fertilizer is made at high pressure because N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ has fewer gas moles on the right.

Pop bottles

Opening a pop lowers pressure, so CO₂ comes out of solution as bubbles.

Blood and oxygen

Haemoglobin picks up O₂ in the lungs and releases it in tissues as the equilibrium shifts.
