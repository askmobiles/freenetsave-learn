Gene regulation and mutations

How cells switch genes on and off (lac and trp operons, eukaryotic control) and how mutations change DNA.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 science.

Learn what you need to know

Every cell has the same DNA, but each cell type uses different genes. Gene regulation controls which genes are expressed, when and how much.

In bacteria, genes for one job are grouped in an operon: a promoter, an operator (on/off switch) and the structural genes. A regulatory gene makes a repressor protein.

🥛lac operon (inducible): normally OFF. Lactose (allolactose) binds the repressor, it falls off the operator, genes turn ON
🧪trp operon (repressible): normally ON. Lots of tryptophan binds the repressor, which then blocks the operator, genes turn OFF
🧬Eukaryotes: chromatin packing, transcription factors and enhancers, RNA processing, mRNA lifespan, protein changes
🏷️Epigenetics: DNA methylation and histone changes turn genes off or on without changing the sequence

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Point mutations: silent (same amino acid), missense (different amino acid, as in sickle-cell anaemia), nonsense (early stop codon). Frameshift mutations (insertion or deletion not in multiples of 3) change every codon after them.

Chromosome mutations include deletion, duplication, inversion and translocation. Mutagens include UV light, X-rays, cigarette smoke chemicals and some viruses. Mutations in germ cells can be inherited; mutations in body cells are not.

Remember: lac = inducible (lactose turns it on); trp = repressible (tryptophan turns it off). Frameshifts are usually the most harmful point mutations.

Key words

  • operon: a group of bacterial genes controlled by one promoter and operator
  • repressor: a protein that blocks transcription by binding the operator
  • frameshift mutation: an insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame
  • mutagen: something that causes mutations, such as UV light

See real-life examples

Sunscreen

UV light damages skin-cell DNA; sunscreen lowers the risk of mutations that can lead to skin cancer.

Sickle-cell anaemia

One base change (missense) in the haemoglobin gene makes red blood cells bend into a sickle shape.

Identical twins

Twins have the same DNA, but epigenetic changes over life make their gene use slightly different.
