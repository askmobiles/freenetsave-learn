Rates, equilibrium and acids

Learn what you need to know

Reaction rate is how fast reactants turn into products. By collision theory, particles must hit with enough energy (the activation energy) and the right orientation.

🌡️Higher temperature: faster particles, more energetic collisions
🧂Higher concentration or pressure: more collisions
🔨More surface area (smaller pieces): more collisions
⚙️A catalyst: lowers activation energy, not used up

In a closed system a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium: forward and reverse rates are equal, so amounts stay constant. The equilibrium constant K = [products]/[reactants], each raised to its coefficient. K > 1 favours products.

Le Châtelier's principle: if a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to reduce the change. Add reactant → shifts right. Raise pressure → shifts toward fewer gas molecules. Heat an endothermic reaction → shifts right.

Acids donate H⁺ (protons); bases accept them (Brønsted–Lowry). pH = −log[H₃O⁺]. pH 7 is neutral at 25 °C; each pH unit is 10 times the [H₃O⁺]. Strong acids (HCl) ionize fully; weak acids (vinegar) only partly. A buffer resists pH change.

Remember: equilibrium is dynamic, not stopped. pH 3 is 10 times more acidic than pH 4.

Key words

  • activation energy: the minimum energy needed for a reaction to happen
  • catalyst: speeds up a reaction without being used up
  • dynamic equilibrium: forward and reverse rates are equal
  • buffer: a solution that resists changes in pH

See real-life examples

Fridge food

Food spoils more slowly in the fridge because the reactions of decay are slower at low temperature.

Car catalytic converters

Platinum and palladium catalysts change toxic CO and NO into CO₂ and N₂ in a car's exhaust.

Your blood

A carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer keeps blood pH near 7.4.
