Special relativity

Einstein's two postulates, simultaneity, time dilation, length contraction, relativistic momentum and E = mc².⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 12 science.

Learn what you need to know

Einstein's special relativity (1905) rests on two postulates: (1) the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames; (2) the speed of light in a vacuum, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, is the same for all observers, however they move.

The Michelson–Morley experiment (1887) found no "ether" that light travels through, which supported the second postulate.

⏳Time dilation: Δt = Δt₀ ÷ √(1 − v²/c²); moving clocks run slow
📏Length contraction: L = L₀√(1 − v²/c²), only along the direction of motion
🔀Simultaneity is relative: events at the same time for one observer may not be for another
⚛️E = mc²: mass is a form of energy

The factor γ = 1 ÷ √(1 − v²/c²) is close to 1 at everyday speeds, so relativity only matters near c. As speed approaches c, momentum and energy grow without limit, so nothing with mass can reach the speed of light.

Evidence: muons made high in the atmosphere live long enough to reach the ground because of time dilation; GPS satellites must correct for relativity or their positions would drift by kilometres a day; particle accelerators confirm relativistic momentum.

Remember: proper time Δt₀ and proper length L₀ are measured in the object's own frame. Moving clocks run slow; moving lengths shrink.

Key words

  • postulate: a basic statement accepted as the starting point of a theory
  • proper time: time measured by a clock moving with the event
  • time dilation: a moving clock is measured to run slow
  • length contraction: a moving object is measured to be shorter along its motion

See real-life examples

GPS in your phone

Satellite clocks must be adjusted for relativity to keep locations accurate.

Cosmic-ray muons

Muons from the upper atmosphere reach the ground only because their clocks run slow at nearly light speed.

Nuclear power

CANDU reactors turn a tiny loss of mass into huge amounts of energy, as E = mc² predicts.
