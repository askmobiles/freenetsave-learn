Energy changes in reactions

Learn what you need to know

Every reaction breaks bonds (needs energy) and makes bonds (releases energy). The overall heat change at constant pressure is the enthalpy change, ΔH.

🔥Exothermic: releases heat, ΔH is negative, surroundings get warmer
🧊Endothermic: absorbs heat, ΔH is positive, surroundings get cooler
⛰️Activation energy: the "hill" reactants must climb
📉Energy diagram: products lower than reactants = exothermic

Calorimetry measures heat: q = mcΔT. For water, c = 4.18 J/(g·°C). Then ΔH = −q ÷ moles reacting (the minus sign because heat gained by the water was lost by the reaction).

Hess's law: the total ΔH is the same no matter which path you take, so you can add equations (and their ΔH values) to find an unknown ΔH. Reverse an equation → change the sign of ΔH; multiply it → multiply ΔH.

Standard enthalpy of formation (ΔH°f) is the ΔH to make 1 mol of a compound from its elements. ΔH°rxn = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants). Elements in their standard state have ΔH°f = 0.

Remember: exo = heat out, ΔH < 0; endo = heat in, ΔH > 0. q = mcΔT.

Key words

  • enthalpy change: the heat released or absorbed by a reaction at constant pressure
  • exothermic: releasing heat to the surroundings
  • calorimetry: measuring heat changes
  • Hess's law: ΔH is the same whatever path the reaction takes

See real-life examples

Hand warmers

Iron powder rusting in a sealed pouch is exothermic and keeps your hands warm on winter days.

Instant cold packs

Ammonium nitrate dissolving in water is endothermic, so the pack feels cold on a sprain.

Home heating

Burning natural gas (methane) releases about 890 kJ per mole, which heats many Canadian homes.
