Learn what you need to know

Structures must hold up against forces. Engineers think about two kinds of forces:

🧱Internal forces act inside a structure
🌬️External forces act on it from outside: wind, snow, people, earthquakes

The main internal forces are:

⬇️⬆️Compression: squeezing or pushing together (a column holding a roof)
⬅️➡️Tension: pulling apart or stretching (a rope in a tug of war, bridge cables)
✂️Shear: forces in opposite directions that make parts slide or tear (scissors)
🔄Torsion: twisting (wringing out a towel)

Structures also carry loads. A dead load is the weight of the structure itself. A live load changes, like people, cars, wind and snow.

Engineers add triangles, arches, beams and strong materials so structures can handle these forces safely.

Remember: compression squeezes, tension stretches, shear slides, torsion twists.

Key words

  • compression: a squeezing force
  • tension: a pulling or stretching force
  • shear: forces pushing in opposite directions that make parts slide
  • torsion: a twisting force
  • live load: weight that changes, like people or snow

See real-life examples

Suspension bridges

On a suspension bridge, the main cables are in tension, and the tall towers are in compression.

Snow on roofs

Heavy snow is a live load. In snowy places like Canada, roofs are built to carry a lot of snow.

Earthquake-safe buildings

In earthquake zones, buildings have flexible frames and braces so they sway instead of breaking.
