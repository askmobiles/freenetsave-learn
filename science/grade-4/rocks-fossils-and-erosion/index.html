Rocks, fossils and erosion

How fossils form and how wind, water and ice shape the land. Grade 4 science.

Learn what you need to know

A fossil is the remains or trace of a living thing from long ago, preserved in rock. Bones, shells, leaf prints and footprints can all become fossils.

1️⃣A plant or animal dies and is buried in mud or sand
2️⃣Soft parts rot; hard parts stay
3️⃣Layers pile on top and press into rock
4️⃣Minerals slowly replace the bone or shell
5️⃣Millions of years later, erosion uncovers it

Weathering is when rocks are broken into smaller pieces by water, ice, wind, plants or chemicals. Water freezing in cracks expands and splits rocks.

Erosion is when those pieces are carried away by water, wind, ice (glaciers) or gravity. Rivers carve valleys, waves shape coastlines, and glaciers left the Great Lakes behind.

Remember: weathering breaks rock, erosion moves it, and fossils tell the story of ancient life.

Key words

  • fossil: the preserved remains or trace of ancient life
  • weathering: breaking rocks into smaller pieces
  • erosion: moving rock and soil to a new place
  • glacier: a huge, slow-moving body of ice

See real-life examples

Alberta dinosaurs

Dinosaur Provincial Park in Alberta has some of the richest dinosaur fossil beds in the world.

Niagara Falls

The Niagara River erodes the rock under the falls, so the falls have moved back upstream over thousands of years.

Potholes in roads

Water seeps into cracks in roads, freezes, expands and breaks the asphalt. That is weathering in action.
