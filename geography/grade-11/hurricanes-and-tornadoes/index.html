Storms: hurricanes and tornadoes

Warm, moist air and spinning winds create the most powerful storms on Earth.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 11 geography.

Learn what you need to know

A hurricane (also called a typhoon or tropical cyclone) forms over warm ocean water of at least about 26.5 °C. Warm, moist air rises, releases heat as it condenses, and the storm spins because of Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect). The calm centre is the eye.

🌀Saffir-Simpson scale: Category 1 (119 km/h winds) to Category 5 (252 km/h or more)
🌊Storm surge — sea water pushed onshore — is often the deadliest part
🍁Hurricane Fiona (2022) was the costliest storm on record in Atlantic Canada

A tornado is a violently rotating column of air that touches the ground, usually from a supercell thunderstorm where warm moist air meets cool dry air. Tornadoes are rated on the Enhanced Fujita (EF) scale from EF0 to EF5. Southern Ontario and the Prairies have some of the most tornadoes in Canada.

Size vs strength: hurricanes are huge (hundreds of km wide) and last days; tornadoes are small (usually under 1 km wide) and last minutes, but can have the strongest winds on Earth.

Key words

  • hurricane: a large rotating storm that forms over warm ocean water
  • storm surge: a rise of sea water pushed onshore by a storm
  • Coriolis effect: the turning of winds caused by Earth's rotation
  • supercell: a powerful rotating thunderstorm that can produce tornadoes
