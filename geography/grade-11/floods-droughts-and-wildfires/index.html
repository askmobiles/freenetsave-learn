Floods, droughts and wildfires

Too much water, too little water and fire are Canada's costliest natural disasters.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 11 geography.

Learn what you need to know

🌊Floods: heavy rain, spring snowmelt, ice jams or storm surge; cities flood more because pavement stops water soaking in
🏜️Droughts: long periods with little rain; crops fail and rivers and reservoirs drop (e.g., the Prairies in the 1930s "Dust Bowl")
🔥Wildfires: need fuel, heat and oxygen; lightning and people start them; hot, dry, windy weather spreads them

Canadian examples: the 2013 Alberta floods (Calgary, about $5 billion damage); Hurricane Hazel (1954) flooded Toronto and killed 81 people, leading to conservation authorities and floodplain rules; the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire forced about 88,000 people to leave; 2023 was Canada's worst wildfire season on record, with about 15 million hectares burned.

Reducing risk: don't build on floodplains; keep wetlands that soak up water; use dikes and floodways (Winnipeg's Red River Floodway); create FireSmart zones by clearing brush around homes; water conservation during droughts.

Natural vs disaster: a flood or fire is a natural event; it becomes a disaster when it harms people and property. Where and how we build changes the risk.

Key words

  • floodplain: low, flat land beside a river that floods naturally
  • drought: a long period of much less rain than normal
  • evacuation: moving people away from danger
  • FireSmart: a Canadian program to reduce wildfire risk around homes and communities
