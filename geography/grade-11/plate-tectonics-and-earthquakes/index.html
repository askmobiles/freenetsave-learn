Plate tectonics and earthquakes

Earth's surface is broken into moving plates. Where they meet, stress builds up and is released as earthquakes.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 11 geography.

Learn what you need to know

Earth's outer shell, the lithosphere, is broken into about 15 major plates that move a few centimetres a year on the softer asthenosphere below. Heat from inside Earth drives this motion through convection.

↔️Divergent boundary: plates pull apart (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland)
➡️⬅️Convergent boundary: plates collide; one may sink (subduct) below the other (Cascadia off B.C., Japan, the Andes)
⇅Transform boundary: plates slide past each other (San Andreas Fault)

An earthquake happens when rocks along a fault suddenly slip. The point underground where it starts is the focus (hypocentre); the point on the surface above it is the epicentre. Size is measured by moment magnitude: each whole number is about 32 times more energy. Undersea quakes can cause tsunamis.

Canada's risk: the Cascadia Subduction Zone off Vancouver Island can produce a magnitude 9 earthquake. The last one was in January 1700.

Key words

  • plate: a large moving piece of Earth's lithosphere
  • subduction: when one plate sinks beneath another
  • epicentre: the point on the surface directly above where an earthquake starts
  • tsunami: a series of large ocean waves usually caused by an undersea earthquake

See real-life examples

B.C.

Schools in British Columbia practise "Drop, Cover and Hold On" earthquake drills.

Iceland

You can stand in a valley where the North American and Eurasian plates pull apart.

Japan

The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (magnitude 9.0) caused a deadly tsunami and a nuclear accident.
