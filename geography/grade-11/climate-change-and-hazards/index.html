Climate change and natural hazards

A warming climate is changing how often and how strongly some natural hazards happen, especially in Canada's North.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 11 geography.

Learn what you need to know

Burning coal, oil and gas adds greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide (CO₂) to the air, trapping more heat. Earth has warmed about 1.2 °C since the late 1800s, and Canada is warming about twice as fast as the world average (the North about three times as fast).

🔥More extreme heat and longer, more intense wildfire seasons
🌧️Heavier downpours and more urban flooding
🧊Melting sea ice and thawing permafrost damage Arctic roads, buildings and hunting routes
🌊Rising sea levels increase coastal flooding and erosion

Example: in June 2021, a heat dome set Canada's record temperature of 49.6 °C in Lytton, B.C.; the next day a wildfire destroyed the village, and hundreds of people in B.C. died from the heat.

Responses: mitigation means cutting emissions (clean electricity, electric transit, efficiency); adaptation means preparing for changes already coming (cooling centres, flood defences, stronger building codes).

Careful wording: climate change does not cause each single storm, but it makes some extreme events, like heat waves and heavy rain, more likely and more severe.

Key words

  • greenhouse gas: a gas such as CO₂ that traps heat in the atmosphere
  • permafrost: ground that stays frozen for at least two years in a row
  • mitigation: actions that reduce the causes of climate change
  • adaptation: actions that prepare for the effects of climate change

See real-life examples

Nunavut

Thawing permafrost is cracking building foundations and roads in northern communities.

Cities

Toronto and other cities open cooling centres during heat warnings.

Coasts

Rising seas threaten the Chignecto Isthmus that links Nova Scotia to New Brunswick.
