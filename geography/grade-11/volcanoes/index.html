Volcanoes

Volcanoes form where magma reaches the surface. Their shape and danger depend on the type of magma.⁠​​‌​‌​​​​‌‌​​​‌‌​​‌​‌​​‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌‌‌​​‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌​‌‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌​‌‌‌​‌​​​‌‌‌​​‌‌​‌‌​​​​‌​‌‌‌​‌‌​​‌‌​​‌​‌⁠ Grade 11 geography.

Learn what you need to know

Most volcanoes form at plate boundaries — especially around the Pacific Ocean in the Ring of Fire — or over hot spots such as Hawaii. Magma that reaches the surface is called lava.

🌋Shield volcano: runny basalt lava, gentle slopes, mostly calm eruptions (Mauna Loa, Hawaii)
🏔️Stratovolcano (composite): sticky, gas-rich magma, steep cone, explosive eruptions (Mount St. Helens, Mount Fuji)
🕳️Caldera: a huge crater formed when a volcano collapses after a giant eruption (Yellowstone)

Hazards: pyroclastic flows (fast avalanches of hot gas and ash — the deadliest), lahars (volcanic mudflows), ash falls that collapse roofs and stop airplanes, and gases. Big eruptions can cool the planet: Tambora (1815) caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816.

Benefits too: volcanic soils are very fertile, and volcanic heat produces geothermal energy (Iceland gets most of its heating from it).

Key words

  • magma: melted rock below the surface
  • lava: magma that reaches the surface
  • pyroclastic flow: a fast-moving avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock
  • hot spot: a place where hot material rises from deep in the mantle, away from plate edges

See real-life examples

Canada

Mount Meager in B.C. erupted about 2,400 years ago and is still considered active.

Travel

In 2010 ash from Iceland's Eyjafjallajökull shut down air travel in Europe for days.

Energy

Iceland heats most homes with geothermal energy.
