How landforms are made

Learn what you need to know

Landforms are shaped by two kinds of forces.

⬆️Building forces (inside Earth): folding, faulting and volcanoes push land up
⬇️Wearing forces (outside): weathering breaks rock; erosion carries it away by water, wind and ice

Folding bends rock layers into fold mountains (Rockies). Faulting cracks and moves blocks of rock. Volcanoes build cones of lava and ash.

Weathering breaks rock into pieces: frost wedging (water freezes in cracks and expands), plant roots, and chemical weathering (acid rain dissolving limestone). Erosion moves the pieces. Deposition drops them somewhere else, forming deltas, beaches and sand dunes.

Glaciers are powerful eroders: they carved the Great Lakes, Ontario's many lakes and U-shaped valleys, and dropped hills of sand and gravel (like the Oak Ridges Moraine).

Young vs old mountains: the Rockies are young and jagged; the Appalachians are old and worn down by hundreds of millions of years of erosion.

Key words

  • weathering: breaking down rock into smaller pieces
  • erosion: moving weathered material by water, wind or ice
  • deposition: dropping eroded material in a new place
  • moraine: a ridge of rock and soil left by a glacier

See real-life examples

Niagara Falls

The falls erode backward about 30 cm a year (it was faster before water was diverted for power).

Potholes in roads

Frost wedging in winter cracks roads, just like it cracks rock.

Oak Ridges Moraine

This ridge north of Toronto was left by glaciers and stores much of the region's groundwater.
