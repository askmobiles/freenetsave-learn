Moving plates

Learn what you need to know

Earth's outer layer (the crust and upper mantle) is broken into about a dozen large tectonic plates. They float on hot, slowly moving rock below and move about 2–10 cm a year — about as fast as your fingernails grow.

↔️Divergent: plates pull apart; magma rises and new crust forms (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, Iceland)
➡️⬅️Convergent: plates push together; mountains form or one plate sinks under another (Himalayas, Andes)
↕️Transform: plates slide past each other; earthquakes happen (San Andreas Fault, California)

Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen along plate boundaries. The Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean has about 75% of the world's volcanoes.

Canada's west coast sits near the boundary where the Juan de Fuca plate sinks under the North American plate, so B.C. can have large earthquakes.

Evidence: the coasts of South America and Africa fit like puzzle pieces, and the same fossils are found on both — they were once joined in a supercontinent called Pangaea.

Key words

  • tectonic plate: a huge slab of Earth's crust and upper mantle that moves slowly
  • plate boundary: where two plates meet
  • Ring of Fire: a ring of volcanoes and earthquakes around the Pacific Ocean
  • Pangaea: a supercontinent that existed about 300–200 million years ago
